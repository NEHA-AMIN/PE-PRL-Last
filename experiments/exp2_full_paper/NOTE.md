# The Role of $w_{ij}$ in Experiment 2 — A Deep Scientific Analysis

## 1. What the Formula Does, Precisely

Before any analysis, nail down exactly what $w_{ij}$ is in the code. In `distance_operator.py`:

```python
# Line 52
d_ij = torch.cdist(X, X, p=1.0)        # [B, L, L] — L1 distance between P_i and P_j

# Line 59
w_ij = 1.0 / (1.0 + d_ij)              # [B, L, L] — feature-space weight

# Line 62
W = alpha.unsqueeze(0) * w_ij          # [B, L, L] — combined weight α(i,j) · w_ij
```

The full **combined weight** used in the displacement sum is:

$$
W_{ij}
=
\alpha(i,j)\cdot w_{ij}
=
\frac{1}{1+|i-j|^a}
\cdot
\frac{1}{1+\|P_i-P_j\|_1}
$$

And the full output for position $i$ is:

$$
O_i
=
\sum_{j\ne i}
W_{ij}\cdot(P_i-P_j)
=
\left(
\sum_{j\ne i}W_{ij}
\right)P_i
-
\sum_{j\ne i}W_{ij}P_j
$$

where

- $\displaystyle\sum_{j\ne i}W_{ij}$ = scalar
- $\displaystyle\sum_{j\ne i}W_{ij}P_j$ = weighted centroid

Without $w_{ij}$, the formula collapses to what Exp 4 and Exp 5 use at `ordering_operator.py:50`:

$$
O_i^{\text{no-}w}
=
\frac{1}{N-1}
\sum_{j\ne i}(P_i-P_j)
=
P_i-\bar{P}
$$

where

$$
\bar{P}
=
\frac{1}{N-1}
\sum_{j\ne i}P_j
$$

is just the uniform mean over other positions. The weight collapses to a constant $\frac{1}{N-1}$ for all $j$.

These are **fundamentally different operators** and the difference is exactly what this analysis needs to dissect.

---

## 2. What $w_{ij}$ Was Intended to Do (The Scientific Motivation)

$$
w_{ij}
=
\frac{1}{1+d_{ij}}
$$

is a **semantic proximity gate**. The theory behind including it is:

> *Positions whose Legendre embeddings are already similar should influence each other's ordering signal more than positions that are very different.*

The intuition is borrowed from **graph-based nearest-neighbor smoothing**: in a $k$-NN graph, you weight each neighbor by inverse distance so that the closest neighbors dominate the aggregation. Applied here, if $\|P_i-P_j\|_1$ is small (positions $i$ and $j$ are close in positional-embedding space), then $w_{ij}\approx1$ (full weight), and the displacement $P_i-P_j$ is small too. If $\|P_i-P_j\|_1$ is large, then $w_{ij}\approx0$ (suppressed weight), and even though $P_i-P_j$ is large, the contribution is zeroed out.

The combined effect of $\alpha(i,j)$ and $w_{ij}$ was supposed to create a **doubly-gated ordering signal**: nearby in sequence index AND nearby in positional space simultaneously receive the most weight. This is an attempt to create an encoding that is simultaneously **temporally local** and **positionally coherent**.

---

## 3. Why $w_{ij}$ Is Mathematically Problematic When Applied to Legendre Embeddings

This is the critical flaw, and it requires careful derivation.

The operator in Exp 2 applies `distance_operator` to `legendre_pos`, not to `value_emb` — this is explicitly stated at `embed.py:155`:

```python
distance_pos = self.distance_operator(legendre_pos)   # input = P, not X_i
```

So $X$ in the operator formula is actually $P$ — the Legendre matrix. Now examine what

$$
d_{ij}
=
\|P_i-P_j\|_1
$$

actually means for Legendre polynomials.

Legendre vectors are generated by `legendre_embedding.py:52`:

$$
\hat{i}
=
\frac{2i}{N-1}-1,
\qquad
P_{i,k}
=
\frac{L_k(\hat{i})}{\sqrt{d}}
$$

For $L=96$ and $d=512$, the positions $\hat{i}$ are evenly spaced in $[-1,1]$. The L1 distance between two Legendre vectors is:

$$
d_{ij}
=
\|P_i-P_j\|_1
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}
\left|
L_k(\hat{i})
-
L_k(\hat{j})
\right|
$$

**Key fact:** This $d_{ij}$ is a **fixed, deterministic, non-trainable function of position indices alone**. It never changes because Legendre embeddings are registered as non-trainable buffers at `legendre_embedding.py:34`:

```python
self.register_buffer('legendre_emb', legendre_emb)
```
So $w_{ij}$ is also fully **deterministic and constant across all training and inference**. It encodes: *"how different are positions $i$ and $j$ in Legendre polynomial space?"*

Now here is the mathematical problem: $d_{ij}$ is already a **strict monotone function** of $|i-j|$ in the Legendre polynomial domain. Positions that are close in index $|i-j|$ also have small $\|P_i-P_j\|_1$, because Legendre polynomials are smooth continuous functions. Positions that are far in index have large $\|P_i-P_j\|_1$.

This means

$$
w_{ij}=\frac{1}{1+d_{ij}}
$$

is **highly correlated** with

$$
\alpha(i,j)=\frac{1}{1+|i-j|^a}.
$$

Both terms suppress long-range pairs and amplify short-range pairs. The combined weight

$$
W_{ij}=\alpha\cdot w
$$

is a product of **two correlated proximity terms** — **the distance is being double-counted.**

To state this precisely:

$$
W_{ij}
=
\frac{1}{1+|i-j|}
\cdot
\frac{1}{1+f(|i-j|)}
$$

where $f$ is a monotone function of $|i-j|$ via the smooth Legendre manifold. Both factors point the same direction, so their product decays faster than either alone. The effective attention window shrinks dramatically. For $L=96$, a pair at $|i-j|=10$ gets roughly $\alpha\approx0.09$ from index decay alone; then $w$ reduces it further. The model effectively only looks at 1–2 adjacent neighbors.

---

# 4. The Critical Self-Cancellation Problem

There is a second, deeper mathematical issue.

With $w_{ij}$ present, expand the Exp 2 full $O_i$:

$$
O_i
=
\sum_{j\ne i}
\frac{1}{1+|i-j|}
\cdot
\frac{1}{1+\|P_i-P_j\|_1}
\cdot
(P_i-P_j)
$$

Now consider what happens for the **middle positions** of the sequence (e.g., $i=48$ for $L=96$). By symmetry of the Legendre domain mapping, the Legendre manifold is symmetric around $\hat{i}=0$. The displacement $P_i-P_{i+k}$ and $P_i-P_{i-k}$ have the same magnitude but point in opposite directions (because Legendre polynomials are either even or odd functions). The weights $W_{i,i+k}$ and $W_{i,i-k}$ are also equal by symmetry.

Therefore, for all middle positions:

$$
O_i^{\mathrm{middle}}
=
\sum_{k=1}^{\min(i,L-1-i)}
W_{i,i+k}(P_i-P_{i+k})
+
W_{i,i-k}(P_i-P_{i-k})
\approx0
$$

The contributions from left and right neighbors cancel! This is the exact same result as the uniform mean (

$$
O_i=P_i-\bar{P}
$$

) for a symmetric sequence — which equals zero at the center. So $w_{ij}$ adds computational cost but produces nearly the same result as the simpler uniform mean for most positions.

For **endpoint positions** ($i=0$ or $i=95$), all neighbors are on one side, so $O_i$ does not cancel — but these are exactly 2 positions out of 96. The overwhelming majority of positions produce near-zero ordering signal, regardless of whether $w_{ij}$ is included.
